Embedding Explorer

An AI model turns a word, a picture, a sound or a video into a list of 768 numbers, called an embedding. Things with similar meanings get similar lists — even when one is a word and the other is a photo. Words with a dotted line under them explain themselves when you point at or tap them.

1. Load the model

The model (Google's EmbeddingGemma 2) runs inside this browser, so what you add stays on this computer. It downloads once and is then remembered.

More detail

Most AI runs on a company's computers: you send your words or pictures away and get an answer back. This one is small enough to run on your own computer. It has a few hundred million learned settings for words, plus separate parts for pictures and sounds that turn them into the same kind of list.

The model was trained by showing it enormous numbers of matching pairs, such as a photo and its caption, and nudging its settings so that matching pairs get similar lists and mismatched pairs get different ones. Nobody told it what any of the 768 numbers should mean; it worked out its own way of using them.

The 4-bit version stores each setting less exactly so the download is smaller. With a graphics chip a word takes about a tenth of a second; without one, words still work but pictures, sounds and videos can take minutes.

How the words are given to the model

The model works best when text starts with a short instruction. Each word or sentence you add is actually sent as:

The first works well for matching words to pictures and sounds; the second for comparing sentences with each other. Try switching and watch the numbers change.

More detail

The same model is used for different jobs: finding the web page that answers a question, finding sentences that mean the same, sorting texts into groups. During training each job had its own instruction in front of the text, so the instruction tells the model which job the list is for. A question like “How old are you?” and its answer “I am twelve” should be close for searching, but “How old are you?” and “What is your age?” should be close for sentence similarity.

Before the model sees any text, the text is cut into tokens: whole words, or pieces of rarer words. The model makes one list per token, lets every token look at all the others, and finally averages them into one list of 768 numbers. That's why a sentence gets one embedding, not one per word.

3. Star view

Each of the numbers is drawn as a ray from the center: green for positive, red for negative, longer for bigger. Pick a second thing to see the difference between the two. Point at a ray to see what it seems to be about.

minus
What do the rays mean?

Point at a ray (or tap it) to see which everyday words have unusually high or low values for that number. These come from a study: the model made embeddings for about 2,800 words, and for each of the 768 numbers we listed the words where it was highest and lowest.

A few numbers lean toward one kind of word, such as music or sports. But most mix unrelated words together: the model doesn't keep one number per idea. The longest rays are different again: they're big for everything, so they say almost nothing about what a thing is.

More detail

For each number, the study found the average value over all 2,800 words, and how much it usually varies. A word is “high” for a number when its value is far above that average. If the words in a group (animals, food, feelings, music and so on) are high on average, the page says that group leans that way. About a third of the numbers have such a leaning group; the others don't.

Why don't the numbers mean one thing each? The model has to fit far more ideas than it has numbers. It seems to store each idea as a direction, a mix of many numbers, and many ideas share each number. Researchers call this superposition, and finding the real directions is an active area of research. The arrows in section 6 are directions like that, which you choose yourself.

The study used words, so the meanings fit pictures and sounds less well. And the six longest rays (numbers 116, 392, 112, 555, 733 and 30) are about the same for every word and picture, but a bit different for sounds, so they partly say what kind of thing it is.

Why do all the stars look alike?

Every embedding this model makes contains a big shared part that is almost the same for a word, a photo or a sound. In the example set, that shared part is about two thirds of each list, and just six positions (such as numbers 112 and 116) carry almost half of it. That is why every star has the same few long rays, and why even “idea” and “horse” come out 0.70 alike.

Tick Take away what everything shares at the top. It subtracts the average of everything you have added, leaving what makes each thing different. The stars stop looking alike, unrelated things score near 0, and related things such as “unicorn” and “horse” stay well above 0. The average depends on what you have added, so adding more things changes it.

Even then, 768 rays are too many to tell apart by eye, which is why the page starts by using the first 256 numbers. Try 128 and the differences are even easier to see.

A few rays may still reach the edge: the rays are scaled to fit 99% of the numbers, and the few bigger ones are cut off.

More detail

Think of each embedding as an arrow in a space with 768 directions. If every arrow also has a long piece pointing the same way, all the arrows end up in a narrow cone, and any two of them look alike. Researchers have found this “cone” in most language models. One explanation is that some numbers act as a kind of background the model uses for its own bookkeeping, not to say anything about meaning.

Taking away the average moves the center of the cone to the middle. Then what's left is how each thing differs from the others on the page, which is what you usually want to see. But the average changes with what's on the page: with only animals, “dog” and “cat” differ in small ways; add some cars and “dog” and “cat” suddenly look closer to each other, because the cars pull the average toward them.

Why can I use fewer numbers?

This model was trained so that the first numbers carry the most important information. Keeping only the first 256 of the 768 loses very little, so the page starts there. Keeping 128 loses more. The setting at the top changes every section.

More detail

The training is called Matryoshka training. While learning, the model was scored not only on how well all 768 numbers worked, but also on how well just the first 512, 256 and 128 worked. So it learned to put the most useful information first, like packing a suitcase with the important things on top.

Shorter lists are cheaper to store and quicker to compare, which matters when a search engine holds billions of them. After cutting a list short, the page scales it back to length 1 so that similarities still run from −1 to 1.

We measured it with 560 everyday words in 20 groups (animals, food, feelings…). Using all 768 numbers, 62% of each word's five nearest words were from its own group. With 256 numbers it was 59%, with 128 it was 53%, and with 64 only 41%.

4. Who is close to whom?

Each square shows the similarity of two things: 1 means the same direction, 0 means unrelated. Darker means closer.

Something to notice

Compare a word with a picture of the same thing, and two words with each other. Words tend to be closer to other words, and pictures to other pictures, even when the meanings match. Researchers call this the modality gap. So look at which picture is closest to a word, not just at the size of the number.

Most squares are between 0.5 and 0.8 because of the part every embedding shares (see “Why do all the stars look alike?” above). Tick Take away what everything shares at the top and the numbers spread out around 0.

The number is the cosine similarity: multiply the two lists number by number and add everything up (each list has been scaled to length 1 first).

More detail

Why multiply and add? Where both lists have big numbers of the same sign, the product is big and positive and pushes the total up. Where one is positive and the other negative, the product is negative and pulls it down. So the total is large when the two lists agree in many places. Because both lists have length 1, the total can't be more than 1 (identical) or less than −1 (exactly opposite).

Similarity isn't the same as “means the same”. “Hot” and “cold” are often close, because they're used in the same kinds of sentences: both describe temperature. In a study with 2,800 words, “cold” was the fourth-nearest word to “hot”, just after “heat”, “warm” and “temperature”.

The modality gap appears because the picture part and the word part of the model start out separate and are only trained to agree on which picture goes with which caption. Nothing in training asks a picture of a dog to be closer to the word “dog” than the word “puppy” is.

5. Embedding arithmetic

Lists of numbers can be added and subtracted: that's embedding arithmetic. What is nearest to the result?

minusplus
    Why does it land on something odd?

    The result is usually nearest its own inputs, so look at what comes after them. And it can only land on things that are on the page. In a study with 2,800 words, tiger − cat + dog landed nearest wolf. With no wolf on the page, the nearest thing is just whatever is left, often with a low score like 0.2: a weak match, not an answer.

    Taking away “cat” also takes away what cats share with other animals. So “horse” and “dog” lose points for being animals, but “house” doesn't. Add “wolf” and try again.

    More detail

    The score of each thing is roughly its similarity to tiger, minus its similarity to cat, plus its similarity to dog. With the first 128 numbers and the shared part taken away, “house” scored 0.15 with tiger, 0.08 with cat and 0.26 with dog, while “horse” scored 0.10, 0.13 and 0.19. Add them up and house wins, 0.33 to 0.16. Nobody knows exactly why “house” is that close to “dog” with only 128 numbers. With all 768 numbers, “horse” beats “house”.

    The famous example is king − man + woman, which lands near queen. In the study it did: with all 768 numbers, “queen” came first, then “women” and “princess”. Try it with the 👑 set. Arithmetic like this works when the difference between two words (man → woman) is a direction the model uses the same way for other pairs (king → queen). It works for some pairs and not others.

    6. Your own 3D space

    Choose three directions, each from one word to another. Everything you added is placed along them. Drag to turn the space.

    → → →
    How the places are worked out

    The direction from “small” to “big” is the embedding of “big” minus the embedding of “small”. A thing's place along that direction is how much its own embedding points the same way. The places are then centered and stretched to fill the box, so only where things are compared with each other matters.

    More detail

    Each embedding has 768 numbers, so it lives in a space with 768 directions, which nobody can picture. Here you pick three directions you care about and look at the space only along those, a bit like a shadow of a 3D object on a wall shows only two of its directions.

    “How much it points the same way” is the same multiply-and-add as similarity. A tiger scores high on small→big if its list has more in common with “big” than with “small”. The three directions you pick may not be at right angles to each other inside the model: “quiet→loud” and “small→big” could partly point the same way, so things that are big may also drift toward loud.

    Try directions that aren't about the senses, like “bad→good”, “old→new” or “boring→exciting”, and see where pictures and sounds land.
