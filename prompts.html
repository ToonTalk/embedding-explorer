Prompts and responses

Every prompt Ken Kahn gave Claude while making Embedding Explorer, with a few sentences on what Claude did in response. The whole app came from these 28 messages, over three days in November 2026.

Ken's prompts are copied exactly, typos and all. The screenshots he attached aren't reproduced here. Messages marked “sent while Claude was working” arrived in the middle of a task. Times are UK time. Summaries written by Claude; the full story is in How it was made.

Day one · November 3

1 · 16:19

I'm wondering if Google's EmbeddingGemma 2 could be used in a web app for school students to explore ideas such as those I explored in https://docs.google.com/document/d/1YPwrWcSGiGNdS7T9YFbp3Lb1abLIdpYSVtvg7ppf6Lk/edit?usp=sharing It could now include images, audio, and video besides text Is the model too big?

Claude answered that it isn't too big. The model has about 740 million settings in three parts (words, pictures and video, sounds). Stored at 4 bits per setting, the whole thing is about a 470 MB download, or 175 MB for words alone. A browser version for Transformers.js already existed that runs on the graphics chip (WebGPU). Claude offered to build a prototype following the old essay.

2 · 17:22

Yes, prototype it

Claude built a single-file web app with the sections that are still there: add things (typing, files, webcam, microphone), see each embedding as a star, compare everything in a grid, do A − B + C, and build a 3D space from three word pairs. It measured the speeds (0.1 s a word, 0.45 s a picture). It found that the compressed model can't run at all without WebGPU, so it added a slower words-only fallback. All seven example pictures and videos landed on the right word, but a dog barking came out nearer “cat”.

Day two · November 4

add a favicon what do you think of changing the default to 128 or 256? is it feasible for you to run a study to determine what some of the star rays "mean"? Claude did all of it:

  • It added a star favicon, and made 256 numbers the default after measuring that it loses little (59% of each word's five nearest neighbors in its own group, against 62% with all 768).
  • It ran a study of 2,834 words to list, for each of the 768 numbers, the words where it is unusually high or low. Pointing at a ray now shows them. About a third of the numbers lean toward one kind of word; most are a mix.
  • It made a much smaller starting set, added buttons that add whole sets, and added a way to remove many things at once.
  • The sea turtle video hung because the video loader never gave up. Claude replaced it with one that times out and offers “Try again”.
  • Technical words got tooltips, and every explanation got a “More detail” version.

7 · 14:44 · sent while Claude was working

the screenshots are meant to accompany a question about why house is higher than horse/dog

Claude reproduced tiger − cat + dog landing on “house”. It happens with 128 numbers and the shared part taken away: taking away “cat” also takes away what all animals share, so “horse” loses points and “house” doesn't. Among 2,800 words with all 768 numbers, the result lands on “wolf”. Claude's first guess, that “house” and “horse” are spelled alike, turned out to be wrong. The app now explains this under “Why does it land on something odd?”.

11 · 15:33

Let's add a new activity between 5 and 6. The idea is to let the user provide their own arithmetic expression. E.g (a+b)/2 would be interesting when a is morning and b is night. I'm sure there are other expressions that would be interesting.

Claude added section 6, “Your own formula”. It understands words, phrases, emoji, pictures and sounds, numbers, + − × ÷, brackets and average(…). Claude tried candidate formulas against about 150 words to choose the examples: (morning + night) / 2 lands on evening, puppy − dog + cat on kitten, Paris − France + Japan on Tokyo. It noticed that dividing a whole formula by 2 changes nothing, because similarity ignores length, and the app says so.

Day three · November 5

17 · 08:47

My old embedding essay relied upon the universal sentence encoder. I assume EmbeddingGemma 2 produces much better embeddings. Can you find some examples where it outperforms?

Claude ran the essay's old model (the TF.js Universal Sentence Encoder) and EmbeddingGemma 2 on the same tests. Given a sentence, one that means the same and a look-alike, the old model chose right 23 times out of 43 and the new one 34. The new model wins clearly on other languages, emoji, words newer than 2018, “not”, and words with two meanings. For single English words they're about even, and both fail at who did what to whom (“The dog bit the man” against “The man bit the dog”).

18 · 08:55

Geoffrey Hinton mentioned in a lecture that man was closer to dog than cat while woman was closer to cat than dog. But when I tried it they were nearly identical.

Claude measured it. Ken was right that the numbers are nearly identical: they differ in the third decimal place, and “man” is in fact a hair closer to “cat”. But in all six male/female pairs tested, the female word leans further toward “cat” than the male word does. Each gap is tiny; the evidence is that they all agree.

22 · 10:14

I think both the guides and the app need to somewhere explain analogies. The guides mention "puppy − dog + cat". When I asked the AI guide (sonnet 5.5) I didn't get a very good answer. Something about how puppy - dog captures the idea of a young version of animal so when "applied" to cat you get kitten. Me: why does puppy − dog + cat produce kitten AI guide: I can't see that result here. "Puppy," "kitten," and this sum aren't on your page yet, so I can't tell you whether it lands on kitten. Nobody can be sure why a result lands where it does, but a few facts may help: * The result can only point to things you've added, so "kitten" has to be added to win. * Subtracting "dog" also takes away what dog shares with other animals, which can shift the result. * The nearest thing is often one of the inputs, so look at the first thing that isn't puppy, dog, or cat. Before you run it, predict: will kitten come first, or something else? Then try it in section 5. ＋ add kitten ▶ try puppy - dog + cat

Claude measured two explanations, which turn out to be the same math. The arrow from dog to puppy and the arrow from cat to kitten point much the same way (similarity 0.46, against 0.05 for random pairs of words). And the order of the results is exactly each word's similarity to puppy, minus its similarity to dog, plus its similarity to cat. Section 5 now explains both, and a new “Show how each score is made” checkbox shows those sums. The guide got the same facts, plus an instruction to explain rather than say nobody can be sure. Both written guides explain word math too.

26 · 10:52

Would it be feasible to have an option of loading the 2800 words you used in testing? The similarity matrix might become too unwieldy and the popup menus for choosing words too but are there other problems?

Claude checked the code and found more problems with adding 2,800 ordinary items: every card draws its own star, the page redraws everything as each word finishes, embedding them all would take about five minutes, the 3D space would be unreadable, and one of the guide's challenge ideas compares every pair of words. It suggested a separate word list instead: shipped already embedded, never shown as cards, and searched only by the nearest-word lists in sections 5 and 6.

27 · 10:55

Yes, build it

Claude built it. A 2.2 MB file holds 2,838 words, each stored as 8-bit numbers (rounding changes a word's numbers by a similarity of less than 0.0004). A checkbox under the lists in sections 5 and 6, “Also search 2,838 everyday words”, adds them to the search, marked 📖. The list is switched off, with a reason, if the student changes the instruction or uses a model it wasn't made with.

28 · 10:56 · sent while Claude was working

What do mean by "instruction" in "The shipped vectors only match the default instruction and the graphics-chip model. If a student changes the instruction..."

The instruction is the short phrase the app puts in front of every word before the model sees it: “dog” is really sent as “task: search result | query: dog”. Students can change it in section 2 under “How the words are given to the model”. Each choice gives slightly different numbers, so the stored word list only matches the default.
