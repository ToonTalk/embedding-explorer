Teacher guide

Embedding Explorer lets students aged about 10 to 16 look inside one of the ideas at the heart of modern AI: turning meaning into lists of numbers. Students add words, pictures, sounds and videos, then compare, draw, add and subtract the lists the AI makes for them.

Students as investigators

The app is meant to be used in a constructionist way, in the tradition of Seymour Papert: students learn most when they build things that matter to them and share them with others. Here, what students build are collections of words and pictures, formulas, 3D spaces, experiments, and claims about how the model behaves, backed by evidence.

This suits the subject unusually well. The model inside the app is a real one, and nobody, including the people who made it, can fully explain why it puts “house” near “dog”, or why it can't tell “The dog bit the man” from “The man bit the dog”. A student who finds a pattern and tests it is doing real research. You don't need to know the answers first: “I don't know. How could we find out?” is a good thing for a teacher to say here.

Everything below, including the lessons, is only an example. Please be creative: borrow parts, reorder them, replace them with your own ideas, and above all follow your students' questions where they lead.

Before you start

Computers and browsers

  • Desktop or laptop computers with Chrome or Edge. The app needs a graphics chip the browser can use (WebGPU), which most computers from the last few years have. Phones and tablets haven't been tested.
  • A large first download. The model is about 473 MB with pictures, videos and sounds (175 MB for words only: untick those boxes before pressing Load). The browser keeps it, so later visits load in a few seconds. On a school network, load it once on each computer before the lesson.
  • Without a graphics chip the web version still works for words (about 0.2 s each), but pictures and sounds are too slow to use.

Privacy

The embedding model runs entirely in the browser: words, photos, recordings and videos students add stay on their computer. The only thing that can leave is what the AI guide sends when a student asks it a question. It sends a text summary of the page: the labels of what's been added and the numbers. It never sends the pictures or sounds themselves. With Gemini Nano, even that stays on the computer. With Claude, Gemini or ChatGPT, it goes to that company. Each time, the guide shows exactly what it was told, under “What the guide was told”.

Starting from the students

The app's example sets are a quick way in, but things students choose themselves are more interesting to them, and more likely to surprise everyone.

  • Their own interests: teams, games, foods, songs, animals, words from a hobby. Which ones does the model think belong together, and do the students agree?
  • Their own languages: the model reads many languages. Students who speak another language at home can test whether their words land next to the English ones, and find where the translation breaks down. They become the class experts.
  • Their own photos and sounds: a photo of a pet, a drawing, a recording of a musical instrument. These stay on the computer.
  • Their own questions: collect what students wonder about after a first ten minutes of play, and let groups choose one to investigate.

Projects students might make

A few ideas, from short to long. Each ends with something students can show others. Most work well in pairs or small groups.

A set that tells a story
Ten to twenty things chosen to show something about the model, such as “things the model thinks are alike that people don't” or “words that are close but opposite”. Present it with the grid as evidence.
A trap gallery
Sentences that fool the model, with the sentence it confused them with. Swapping who does what, idioms, sarcasm and “not” are good places to look. Groups swap galleries and try to explain each other's traps.
A space of their own
Three directions in section 7 that sort something students care about (scary → cute, cheap → expensive, old → new). Does the class agree with where things landed? Where it doesn't, whose sense of the words is different, the model's or the students'?
A formula zoo
Formulas that work, formulas that fail, and formulas that fail in funny ways, each with an explanation. “Show how each score is made” in section 5 lets students explain any result from the grid's numbers.
Testing a claim
Take a claim about AI, from the news, an expert, or the AI guide, and turn it into an experiment. Geoffrey Hinton's remark about dogs, cats, men and women (Mission 8 in the student guide) is a worked example: the effect turns out to be tiny but consistent. What would count as evidence for or against?
Then and now
The Then and now set shows sentences a 2018 model got wrong. Students look for new sentences that still fool today's model, and guess what the next model will fix.
A better explanation
Ask the 🧭 Guide to explain something, check its answer against the numbers, and write a better explanation for next year's class.

Habits worth encouraging

  • Predict first. Before pressing anything, write down what you expect. A wrong prediction is the most useful kind.
  • Keep a notebook of predictions, results and surprises. It becomes the evidence for whatever students present.
  • Treat surprises as bugs to investigate, not as failures. Why did it land there? What could you change to find out?
  • Check it another way. Does a finding still hold with 768 numbers instead of 256, with the shared part taken away, or with a different set of words?
  • Share and build on each other's work. Short demos to the class, then other groups try to break or extend the finding.

Ideas students are likely to meet

Students who explore usually run into these big ideas, in their own order and their own words. They are useful for noticing and naming what students find, not a checklist to work through.

  1. An AI model can represent the meaning of words, pictures and sounds as lists of numbers, called embeddings, in one shared space.
  2. Similar meanings give similar lists, and similarity can be measured with a single number.
  3. You can do arithmetic on meanings, and it sometimes works (puppy − dog + cat lands on kitten) and sometimes doesn't.
  4. The individual numbers mostly don't mean anything on their own: ideas are spread across many of them.
  5. The model can be wrong in surprising ways, and the way to find out is to make predictions and test them.

One possible sequence: three lessons

This is just an example. It is one way to give a class shared experiences to build on. Feel free to reorder it, cut it, stretch it over more sessions, swap any part for a project above, or skip it entirely and start from a single open question. You and your students will think of better activities than these.

Each lesson is about 45 minutes. The student guide has the missions mentioned below.

Lesson 1 · Meaning as numbers

  • Start with no computers: give pairs of words (dog/cat, dog/apple, hot/cold) and ask students to rank how “close” they are. Is hot closer to cold or to warm?
  • Load the app. Mission 1 (who is close to whom). Compare the class's rankings with the model's.
  • Discuss: what could it mean for two lists of numbers to be “close”? Introduce similarity as “pointing the same way”.
  • To finish, each group makes a small set of their own that they predict the model will group in a surprising way, and tests it.

Lesson 2 · Looking inside

  • Mission 2 (why the stars look alike) and Mission 3 (what a ray means).
  • Discuss: if no single number means “animal”, where is the idea of an animal stored? Use the study directions above the star (one → many, now → past) to show an idea spread over many numbers.
  • Mission 6 (pictures and sounds): students find where the model gets it wrong.

Lesson 3 · Arithmetic with meaning

  • Missions 4 and 5 (word math and inventing formulas). Ask each group to find one formula that works and one that fails, and to explain both.
  • Mission 7 (building a 3D space).
  • Mission 8 (testing Geoffrey Hinton's dogs-and-cats claim). A good way into how models pick up habits from the text they learn from, and why a tiny effect can still be real when many cases agree.
  • Mission 9 (then and now): how a 2018 model and this one handle other languages, “not”, newer words and word order. Good for discussing how fast AI has changed, and what is still hard.
  • Optional: Mission 10, checking the AI guide. A good discussion of trusting AI.

Background: how word math works

puppy - dog + cat is the analogy “puppy is to dog as ? is to cat”. Two explanations help, and they are the same math:

  • Arrows. puppy − dog is an arrow from dog to puppy, roughly “younger”. Following it from cat lands near kitten because the cat → kitten arrow points much the same way. Measured with 256 numbers, the two arrows have a similarity of 0.46. Arrows between random pairs of words average 0.05. For king − man + woman it's 0.45. For tiger − cat + dog (dog → wolf against cat → tiger) it's only 0.19, which is why that one is unreliable.
  • Adding up scores. The order of the results is exactly each thing's similarity to puppy, minus its similarity to dog, plus its similarity to cat. Students can check this with the grid in section 4, or tick “Show how each score is made” in section 5. With 256 numbers: kitten 0.79 − 0.75 + 0.91 = 0.95. “Pet” gets 0.84 − 0.89 + 0.87 = 0.82: it is penalized for being close to dog. Cat itself also gets 0.95, which is why inputs often top the list.

Students can discover the second view for themselves. Before showing them the checkbox, ask whether they can predict an arithmetic result using only the grid.

Misunderstandings worth discussing

These come up often. Rather than correcting them up front, let students run into them, then design a test together.

“A similarity of 0.7 means they're 70% the same.”
Not quite. Without “Take away what everything shares”, almost every pair scores between 0.5 and 0.85, because two thirds of every list is a shared part. Compare rankings, not raw numbers.
“Similar means the same meaning.”
Opposites are often close. In a study of 2,800 words, “cold” was the fourth-nearest word to “hot”, because both are about temperature.
“The arithmetic found the answer.”
It can only land on things already on the page. With nothing better to land on, the top answer may score as low as 0.2: a weak match, not an answer.
“The model knows that a kitten is a young cat.”
Not in the way people do. It learned from how words are used, and that left the dog → puppy and cat → kitten arrows pointing roughly the same way (a similarity of 0.46, where 1 would be identical). That's enough to make kitten win, but the arrows are far from parallel, and for many pairs they don't line up at all.
“Each number stands for a feature, like size or color.”
Only about a third of the numbers even lean toward one kind of word, and none carries much of an idea like “plural”. The ray explanations in the app say this plainly.
“The guide is always right.”
It isn't, especially with the small Gemini Nano model. The guide is told not to invent reasons, but it sometimes does. Treat it as a classmate with ideas, not an authority.

* From a study of 2,800 words using all 768 numbers, with the shared part taken away, which lowers every score. With the first 128 numbers and only the starter examples, tiger − cat + dog lands on “house”: taking away “cat” also takes away what every animal shares. The app explains this under “Why does it land on something odd?”.

  • Then and now: given a sentence, one that means the same and a look-alike, the 2018 Universal Sentence Encoder (used in the 2023 essay) chose right in 23 of 43 tests and this model in 34. The old model fails all seven in the Then and now set. This model gets six; neither can tell “The dog bit the man” from “The man bit the dog”. Details are in study/then-and-now.
  • Dogs, cats, men and women: in the grid man and woman score almost the same with dog and cat (about 0.77 each with 256 numbers; man is in fact a hair closer to cat). But in all six pairs we tried (man/woman, men/women, boy/girl, male/female, father/mother, king/queen) the female word leaned further toward cat, by between 0.002 and 0.03. Each gap alone is no bigger than between random words; the agreement is the evidence. The old Universal Sentence Encoder agreed in only four of the six.
  • Why the stars look alike: about 66% of each list is shared, and six of the 768 numbers hold 44% of that shared part.
  • Pictures and video: 7 of 7 example pictures and videos landed nearest the right word. The dog barking sound landed slightly nearer “cat” than “dog”, with 4-bit and 8-bit models alike.
  • Fewer numbers: of each everyday word's five nearest neighbors, 62% were in its own group with all 768 numbers, 59% with 256, 53% with 128 and 41% with 64.
